Harbour city rebuilds its ferry network, one slow morning at a time

By A. Reporter · Transport Desk · 8 min read

The first ferry leaves at 5:42 a.m., and by then the terminal has already been awake for an hour. Deckhands hose down the gangways, the coffee cart rolls out from under its tarpaulin, and a small crowd of commuters forms in the same shape it has taken for thirty years — a loose crescent facing the water, everyone watching for the same set of running lights to round the point.

What is different this year is the boat. After a decade of deferred maintenance and three separate reviews, the city has begun replacing its ageing diesel fleet with a set of hybrid-electric vessels that charge at the dock between crossings. The first two entered service in March. Four more arrive before the end of the year.

A network built by accident

The ferry network was never designed so much as accumulated. Routes were added when a suburb grew, cut when a bridge opened, and reinstated when the bridge filled up. The result is a timetable that makes sense only to the people who use it daily, and a fleet in which no two boats share the same spare parts.

"We were running a museum and calling it a transport system," said one operations manager, who has worked the harbour for twenty-two years. "Beautiful boats. Terrible spreadsheets."

The replacement programme is as much an exercise in standardisation as it is in decarbonisation. Every new vessel shares a hull, a berth footprint, and a charging interface. Crews trained on one can work any of them. Maintenance windows shrink from days to hours.

What the timetable can't show

Ridership numbers tell one story: a 14 percent increase on the two upgraded routes since March, most of it in the off-peak shoulder hours. The quieter story is on the boats themselves. Without the low diesel rumble, passengers talk. The upper deck, historically the domain of people avoiding conversation, has become the loudest part of the vessel.

Not everyone is convinced. Operators on the outer routes point out that charging infrastructure has been installed at only four of the eleven terminals, which means the new vessels are effectively confined to the inner harbour. The longer crossings — the ones where fuel costs actually bite — remain on the old fleet indefinitely.

The next five years

The transport authority's own projections assume full fleet replacement by 2031, contingent on a funding round that has not yet been approved. Officials describe the timeline as "ambitious but achievable," a phrase that has appeared in each of the three prior reviews.

For now the 5:42 runs on schedule. The crescent of commuters forms, the running lights round the point, and the boat comes in quietly enough that the gulls don't bother moving.

Whether that quiet reaches the outer terminals depends less on engineering than on the budget papers due in the spring — and on whether a city that built its network by accident can bring itself to plan one on purpose.
